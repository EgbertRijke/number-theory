\section{Reduction to the Primes}

\begin{theorem}[Fermat's Two-Square Theorem]
  A natural number $n$ is presentable as the sum of two squares if and only if its square-free part is a product of primes congruent to $1$ modulo $4$.
\end{theorem}

We will give three proofs of Fermat's Two-Square Theorem in the sections following this one. In the current section we will prove some statements facilitating these three proofs.

\begin{theorem}
  Consider two nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$. If any two of the nonnegative integers $a$, $b$, and $ab$ can be written as the sum of two squares, then so can the third.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  If $a=x^2+y^2$ and $b=u^2+v^2$, then the Brahmagupta--Fibonacci identity (\cref{exercise:brahmagupta-fibonacci-identity}) gives us that
  \begin{equation*}
    (x^2+y^2)(u^2+v^2) = (xu\pm yv)^2 + (xv\mp yu)^2.
  \end{equation*}
  This establishes $ab$ as a sum of two squares.

  It remains to show that if $b$ and $ab$ are sums of two squares, then so is $a$. 
\end{proof}

\section{The Method of Brillhart--Hermite--Serret}

Charles Hermite and Joseph-Alfred Serret observed independently in the same year \cite{hermite1848,serret1848}, that there is a remarkable connection between Fermat's problem of expressing a prime $p$ congruent to $1$ modulo $4$ and Euclid's algorithm for finding the greatest common divisor. Their method was later improved by John Brillhart in \cite{brillhart1972}, which we will follow here.

The method of Brillhart--Hermite--Serret starts by making the following observation. Consider first a pair $(a,b)$ of relatively prime integers with $a>b>0$, and use Euclid's algorithm to find a corresponding sequence $(q_0,\ldots,q_N)$ of positive integers, as described in \cref{theorem:correspondence-theorem-quotients-euclidean-algorithm}, and form the sequence
\begin{equation*}
  (q_N,\ldots,q_0,q_0,\ldots,q_N).
\end{equation*}
This sequence of quotients determines, again via \cref{theorem:correspondence-theorem-quotients-euclidean-algorithm}, a pair $(c,d)$ of relatively prime integers with $c>d>0$.

Consider the prime $p:= 521$, which is clearly congruent to $1$ modulo $4$. By fast-exponentiation one checks that $2^{260}\equiv -1\pmod{521}$, so that
\begin{equation*}
  (235)^2\equiv (2^{130})^2 \equiv -1\pmod{521}.
\end{equation*}
Since the number $521$ is prime, the greatest common divisor of $521$ and $235$ is $1$. Nevertheless, we will run Euclid's algorithm:
\begin{align*}
  521 & = 2\cdot 235 + 51 \\
  235 & = 4\cdot 51 + 31 \\
  51 & = 1\cdot 31 + 20 \\
  31 & = 1\cdot 20 + 11 \\
  20 & = 1\cdot 11 + 9 \\
  11 & = 1\cdot 9 + 2 \\
  9 & = 4\cdot 2 + 1 \\
  2 & = 2\cdot 1 + 0.
\end{align*}
There are some special things to observe here. Let us write $a_i=q_ib_i+r_i$ for the $i$th equation, where $0\leq i\leq 7$. For starters, the sequence of $q_i$s is palindromic, which means that it reads the same forwards and backwards. More significantly, the number $b_i^2+b_{8-i}^2$ is always a multiple of $521$:
\begin{align*}
  235^2 + 1^2 & = 106 \cdot 521 \\
  51^2 + 2^2 & = 5 \cdot 521 \\
  31^2 + 9^2 & = 2 \cdot 521 \\
  20^2 + 11^1 & = 1 \cdot 521.
\end{align*}
Exclam! We have expressed $521$ as a sum of squares!
