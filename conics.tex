# Conic Sections (Degree‐2 Curves)

Conic sections are the plane curves defined by a degree‐2 equation
\$Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0\$.  Geometrically they arise by intersecting a plane with a double cone.  The three nondegenerate types are **ellipse** (including the circle), **parabola**, and **hyperbola**; degenerate cases include pairs of lines or a double line (see below).  For example, standard forms include the circle \$x^2+y^2=a^2\$, ellipse \$x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2=1\$, parabola \$y=ax^2\$, and hyperbola \$x^2/a^2 - y^2/b^2=1\$.  Over \$\Bbb Q\$, any smooth conic (genus 0 curve) can be transformed to a diagonal form \$a x^2+b y^2+c z^2=0\$ in projective coordinates.  A key invariant is the discriminant \$B^2-4AC\$: if \$B^2-4AC<0\$ (e.g. circle/ellipse), \$=0\$ (parabola), or \$>0\$ (hyperbola).

Each smooth conic is a “genus 0” curve (topologically a sphere). If a real conic has any rational point, all its rational points can be parametrized by lines through that point.*

Conics are precisely the plane algebraic curves of genus 0.  Over an algebraically closed field, every smooth conic is isomorphic to the projective line \$\Bbb P^1\$, but over \$\Q\$ a conic may or may not have \$\Q\$-points.  For example \$x^2+y^2=3\$ has no rational solutions.  The **Hasse–Minkowski theorem** (the Hasse principle for quadratic forms) tells us that a quadratic equation has a nontrivial rational solution if and only if it has one over \$\R\$ and over \$\Q\_p\$ for every prime \$p\$.  In particular, one checks solvability of a conic by local congruence tests.  (A classical special case is Legendre’s theorem of 1785: the equation \$aX^2+bY^2+cZ^2=0\$ has a rational point iff \$a,b,c\$ are not all the same sign and each of \$-bc,-ac,-ab\$ is a quadratic residue mod the remaining coefficient.)

If a smooth conic \$C\$ **does** have one rational point \$P\$, it has infinitely many: one obtains a rational parametrization by drawing every line (with rational slope) through \$P\$ and finding its other intersection with \$C\$.  This realizes \$C(\Q)\$ in bijection (up to finitely many points) with \$\Q\$ itself.  Concretely, the unit circle \$x^2+y^2=1\$ has the well-known parameterization

$$
(x,y)=\Bigl(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\,\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\Bigr),\quad t\in\Q,
$$

which produces all Pythagorean triples.  In fact **any** smooth conic with one rational point is (over \$\Q\$) birational to the line, so \$C(\Q)\cong\Bbb P^1(\Q)\$.  By contrast, if there are no \$\Q\$-points then \$C(\Q)\$ is empty.

## Rational Diophantine Equations on Conics

Conics give the first interesting examples of Diophantine equations (polynomials in two variables).  Some key examples:

* **Sum of squares:**  \$x^2+y^2=N\$.  Determining which integers \$N\$ are representable as \$x^2+y^2\$ is a classical number‐theory problem.  For primes, Fermat’s theorem states \$p=x^2+y^2\$ (with \$p\$ prime) if and only if \$p\equiv1\pmod4\$.  More generally, the **sum of two squares theorem** says an integer \$N>0\$ is a sum of two squares iff every prime factor \$p\equiv3\pmod4\$ appears to an even exponent in \$N\$.  Viewed as conics, each equation \$x^2+y^2=N\$ (after projectivizing) is a genus-0 curve; if it has a rational point then all solutions can be parametrized.

* **Pell’s Equation:**  \$x^2-Dy^2=1\$ (with \$D>0\$ a nonsquare integer) is the famous Pell–Fermat equation.  Graphically it is a hyperbola.  Lagrange proved that whenever \$D\$ is not a perfect square, this equation has infinitely many integer (hence rational) solutions.  One nontrivial solution (called the *fundamental solution*) generates all others.  (Equivalently, \$(x+y\sqrt{D})\$ is a unit in \$\Z\[\sqrt{D}]\$ of infinite order.)  For example when \$D=2\$, the smallest solution is \$(3,2)\$, and then \$(3+2\sqrt2)^n\$ yields all others.  Modern proofs use continued fractions for \$\sqrt{D}\$ or Minkowski’s lattice‐point theorem.  Indeed, Minkowski’s convex-body argument easily shows *existence* of at least one nontrivial solution for any non-square \$D\$, and thus (together with the norm‐unit group structure) proves infinitude of solutions.

The trivial solution \$(1,0)\$ and the fundamental nontrivial solution \$(3,2)\$ are shown; all other solutions lie on this curve. The existence of infinitely many solutions (Lagrange’s theorem) follows from continued fractions or Minkowski’s geometry-of-numbers argument.*

* **General conics:**  More generally one studies equations like \$ax^2 + bxy + cy^2 + dx + ey + f=0\$ in integers or rationals.  After completing squares and linear changes (over \$\Q\$), such equations can often be reduced to simpler forms.  For instance, \$ax^2+by^2=c\$ can often be studied by modular arithmetic and Legendre’s criterion.  The theory of **binary quadratic forms** provides a unified approach: the homogeneous equation \$ax^2+bxy+cy^2 = N\$ represents \$N\$ if and only if certain congruence conditions (modulo primes) are met.  As one example, \$x^2 + 3y^2 = p\$ (with \$p\$ prime) has solutions iff \$p\equiv1\pmod3\$, etc.  In general, Gauss showed that integral binary quadratic forms of a given discriminant form a finite abelian **class group**, corresponding to the ideal class group of the quadratic field \$\Q(\sqrt{D})\$.  Thus sums of squares, representations by \$x^2+2y^2\$, \$x^2-2y^2\$, etc., all become cases of the theory of quadratic forms.

## The Hasse (Local–Global) Principle

A cornerstone result for conics is that **local solvability implies global solvability**.  Equivalently, a conic over \$\Q\$ has a rational point if and only if it has a point over \$\R\$ and over \$\Q\_p\$ for every prime \$p\$.  This is part of the *Hasse–Minkowski theorem* for quadratic forms.  In practical terms, one checks the equation modulo various primes and over the reals.  For example, \$x^2+y^2=3\$ has no \$\Z\$ or \$\Q\$ solution because mod \$3\$ one sees \$X^2+Y^2\equiv0\$ has only the trivial solution mod \$3\$ – failing a local test.  More systematically, **Legendre’s theorem** (1785) gave specific congruence criteria for \$aX^2+bY^2+cZ^2=0\$ to have nonzero integer solutions; this classical result is a special case of the general local-global principle.

Concretely, to decide \$ax^2+by^2+c z^2=0\$ has a \$\Q\$-solution, one needs: (i) not all \$a,b,c\$ of the same sign (so there is a real solution), and (ii) for each prime \$p\$, the equation has a solution mod \$p\$ (checked by quadratic reciprocity or simpler tests).  Hasse–Minkowski guarantees this criterion is necessary and sufficient.

## Pell’s Equation and Units in Quadratic Fields

The case \$x^2-Dy^2=1\$ deserves special emphasis.  Its solutions correspond to units in the ring of integers of the real quadratic field \$\Q(\sqrt{D})\$.  By Dirichlet’s unit theorem, this unit group is infinite cyclic (for \$D>1\$), which matches the infinite Pell solutions.  A typical proof constructs the *fundamental unit* \$\epsilon = x\_1+y\_1\sqrt{D}>1\$ (for the least solution \$x\_1>1\$) and shows \$(x\_k+y\_k\sqrt{D}) = \epsilon^k\$ are all solutions.  Continued fractions for \$\sqrt{D}\$ explicitly find \$\epsilon\$.

Minkowski’s geometry-of-numbers gives another proof: by considering the lattice of points in \$\R^2\$ corresponding to \$\Z\[\sqrt{D}]\$, one applies the convex-body theorem to show there is a nontrivial lattice point in a suitable region of area \$>4\$, forcing a solution to \$x^2-Dy^2=1\$.  In any case, the result is that **for any nonsquare \$D>0\$, the Pell equation has a nontrivial solution and hence infinitely many**.

## Conics and Geometry of Numbers

More broadly, the **geometry of numbers** (Minkowski theory) plays a key role in the arithmetic of conics.  For real quadratic fields \$K=\Q(\sqrt{D})\$, one uses lattice arguments to bound regulators and class numbers.  Minkowski’s theorems guarantee existence of small solutions to norm equations and give explicit bounds on fundamental units.  For example, a corollary is that every quadratic form \$ax^2 + bxy + c y^2\$ with \$\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert + \lvert c\rvert\$ large enough has a nontrivial integer solution (so genus 0 forms become isotropic).  These ideas underpin algorithms in computational number theory for solving conics.

Historically, Gauss initiated the study of binary forms in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, and later Minkowski’s convex-body theorem (1896) was used to solve Diophantine problems including a new proof of Pell’s theorem.  Cassels’s book *Rational Quadratic Forms* (1978) covers these topics in depth.

## Cryptographic Applications

Though less famous than elliptic curves, conics have found some cryptographic use.  In particular, the set of rational (or finite-field) points on the **Pell hyperbola** \$x^2-Dy^2=1\$ forms an abelian group under the “Brahmagupta” composition law

$$
(x_1,y_1)\otimes (x_2,y_2) = (x_1x_2 + D y_1y_2,\; x_1y_2 + x_2y_1).
$$

Over a finite field \$\F\_q\$, this group is cyclic of order \$q-\chi(D)\$ (where \$\chi\$ is the Legendre symbol).  Recent work has proposed discrete-log–based schemes (analogous to ElGamal) using this group of a Pell conic.  In fact, one can embed ElGamal encryption directly on the Pell hyperbola, often with efficiency benefits.  More generally, any rational conic with a group law (one can even define a “tangent-chord” addition like for elliptic curves) could yield a public-key system.  These applications exploit that conics are easy to compute with but (if done over a large finite field) still have hard discrete-log problems.

Conics also appear indirectly in cryptography via quadratic forms and class groups.  For example, the difficulty of factoring (or computing class numbers) can be related to finding solutions of certain norm equations.  Some *identity-based encryption* and *digital signature* schemes have been proposed using Pell equations and norm form equations.  (For more, see \[Gessica Alecci *et al.*, *Pell Hyperbolas in DLP-based Cryptosystems*].)

## From Conics to Elliptic Curves

Studying conics is the natural “first step” toward the arithmetic of higher-genus curves.  Conics (genus 0) have completely understood rational-point theory: either none, or infinitely many and parametrizable.  The next case is **genus 1** – elliptic curves.  Elliptic curves are cubic equations in two variables (like \$y^2=x^3+Ax+B\$) whose set of rational points \$E(\Q)\$ forms a finitely generated abelian group (Mordell’s theorem).  Just as the Hasse principle solves conics, elliptic curves introduce deep new phenomena.  The **Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture** connects the rank of \$E(\Q)\$ (number of independent rational points) to the behavior of its \$L\$-function at \$s=1\$.  In a sense, conics are “degenerate” elliptic curves (genus 0 instead of genus 1), and techniques like descent and \$L\$-series have analogues for Pell conics.  Conics thus provide a valuable playground: for genus 0 one has explicit parametrizations, while genus 1 curves (elliptics) already exhibit rich arithmetic and famous open problems (like BSD).

Understanding conics paves the way to elliptic curves: it teaches us about Diophantine methods (parametrization, descent), local-global principles, and links between forms and field arithmetic.  In modern terms, a complete algorithm exists to find all rational points on a conic, but no such algorithm is known for elliptic curves in general.  This is why conics are often called the *“poor man’s elliptic curves”*: they share many structures but in a simpler setting.

## Further Reading

For more on these topics, see:

* J.W\.S. Cassels, *Rational Quadratic Forms* (1978, 2\supnd{} ed.); a classic text covering conics, forms, and class groups.
* D.A. Cox, *Primes of the Form \$x^2+ny^2\$* (John Wiley, 1989); discusses sum-of-squares and quadratic forms in depth.
* J.H. Silverman & J. Tate, *Rational Points on Elliptic Curves* (Springer, 1992); Chapter I gives an elementary introduction to conics and Diophantine methods as a stepping stone to elliptic curves.
* B. Poonen, *Computing Rational Points on Curves*, in *Number Theory for the Millennium III* (2002); a survey of genus 0 and 1 curves (see especially the discussion of conics and parametrization).
* F. Lemmermeyer, *Pell Conics: An Alternative Approach to Elementary Number Theory* (2009); and *Conics – A Poor Man’s Elliptic Curves* (2003); preprints exploring the analogy between Pell equations and elliptic curves.
* H. Cohen, *A Course in Computational Algebraic Number Theory* (Springer, 1993); contains background on quadratic fields, units, and applications of the geometry of numbers.
* K. Ireland & M. Rosen, *A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory* (Springer); covers conic Diophantine equations like \$x^2+y^2=N\$ and Pell’s equation with classical proofs.
* N. Koblitz, *A Course in Number Theory and Cryptography* (Springer, 2\supnd{} ed.), Chapter 5; gives an overview of Pell’s equation and related cryptographic applications.

**Sources:** Standard references in number theory as above.  Key facts are supported by classical theorems (Legendre’s criterion, Hasse–Minkowski, Lagrange’s theorem on Pell) and recent surveys.
