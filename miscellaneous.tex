\section{Representing Primes with Homogeneous Cyclotomic Polynomials}

Note that the homogenized cyclotomic polynomial $\tilde{\Phi}_n(X,Y)$ is given by
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde{\Phi}_n(X,Y):= Y^{\varphi(n)}\Phi_n(\tfrac{X}{Y}).
\end{equation*}
For example, $\Phi_3(X) = X^2+X+1$, so $\tilde{\Phi}_3(X,Y) = X^2+XY+Y^2$

\begin{theorem}
  Consider an odd prime $p\neq 3$. The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item The prime $p$ can be written in the form $p=x^2+xy+y^2$.
  \item The congruence $x^2+x+1\equiv 0\pmod{p}$ has a solution.
  \item $-3$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$.
  \item $p\equiv 1\pmod{3}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}
  Consider an odd prime $p>3$. The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item The prime $p$ can be written in the form $p=x^2-xy+y^2$.
  \item The congruence $x^2-x+1\equiv 0\pmod{p}$ has a solution.
  \item $-3$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$.
  \item $p\equiv 1\pmod{3}$.
  \end{enumerate}  
\end{theorem}

\section{Stickelberger's Theorem}

Fix an odd prime $p$, and let $\zeta:=e^{2\pi i/p}$ where $i$ is a primitive root modulo $p$. Set
\begin{equation*}
  K:=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta),\qquad \mathcal{O}_K:=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta].
\end{equation*}
Denote by $\sigma_a:e^{2\pi k/p}\mapsto e^{2\pi ak/p}$ for $1\leq a\leq p-1$ the usual isomorphism
\begin{equation*}
  (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times \to \mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}).
\end{equation*}
\begin{enumerate}
\item There is a unique prime of $\mathcal{O}_K$ lying above $p$, namely
  \begin{equation*}
    \mathfrak{p}:=(1-\zeta)\subset \mathcal{O}_K
  \end{equation*}
  and write $\mathfrak{p}_a:=\sigma_a(\mathfrak{p})$ for its conjugates.
\item Set
  \begin{equation*}
    G_t := -\sum_{x=1}^{p-1}\zeta^{x^t}\in\mathcal{O}_K.
  \end{equation*}
  This Gauss sum $G_t$ measures how uniformly the $t$th powers are distributed modulo $p$. The set of $t$th powers is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$. 
\end{enumerate}
\begin{theorem}
  For every integer $1\leq t\leq p-1$ one has the ideal identity
  \begin{equation*}
    (G_t) = \prod_{a=1}^{p-1}\mathfrak{p}_a^{\lfloor at/p\rfloor},
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

\section{Emma Lehmer's Congruence}

Let
\begin{equation*}
  q_p(2):=\frac{2^{p-1}-1}{p}
\end{equation*}
be the second Fermat quotient modulo $p$.

\begin{theorem}[Emma Lehmer]
  For any odd prime $p$ we have the congruence
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\frac{1}{j}\equiv -2 q_p(2)+ pq_p(2)^2\pmod{p^2}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

More generally, let
\begin{equation*}
  q_n(2):=\frac{2^{\phi(n)}-1}{n}
\end{equation*}
be the second Euler quotient modulo an odd number $n$.

\begin{theorem}[Tianxin Cai]
  For any odd number $n$ we have the congruence
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_{\substack{j=1\\ \gcd(j,n)=1}}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{j}\equiv -2 q_n(2)+ nq_n(2)^2\pmod{n^2}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

\section{Wolstenholme's Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Babbage's congruence]
  For any prime $p\geq 3$, we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p^2}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

In fact, Wolstenholme's Theorem shows that
\begin{theorem}
  \begin{equation*}
    \binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p^3}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

Wolstenholme's theorem has the following equivalent formulation:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{k}\equiv 0\pmod{p^2}.
\end{equation*}

This leads to the concept of a Wolstenholme prime, which is a prime such that
\begin{equation*}
  \binom{2p-1}{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p^4}.
\end{equation*}

Equivalently, a Wolstenholme prime is a prime such that the numerator of the harmonic number $H_{p-1}$ is divisible by $p^3$. Here,
\begin{equation*}
  H_n:=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{1}{k}.
\end{equation*}

Granville showed that
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{k}\equiv 0\pmod{p^3}
\end{equation*}
if and only if $p$ is a Wolstenholme prime.

\section{Fermat's Last Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Mason--Stothers Theorem]
  Consider three relatively prime polynomials $f(x)$, $g(x)$, and $h(x)$ over a field, such that $f(x)+g(x)=h(x)$ and not all of them have vanishing derivative. Then we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \max(\deg(f(x)),\deg(g(x)),\deg(h(x))) \leq \deg(\rad(f(x)g(x)h(x)))-1.
  \end{equation*}
  Here, $\rad(p(x))$ is the product of the distinct irreducible factors of the polynomial $p(x)$. 
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}

    w_0 & = 0, \\

    w_2w_3 & \neq 0, \\

  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

\section{Central Delannoy Numbers}

Recall that the \emph{central Delannoy number}

$$
D_n \;=\;\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}
$$

counts lattice-paths from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$ using steps

$$
\text{East }E=(1,0),\quad
\text{North }N=(0,1),\quad
\text{Diagonal }D=(1,1).
$$

Below are two very different proofs that for

$$
p\text{ an odd prime},\quad n=\frac{p^2-1}{8},\quad
f(n)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+k}k2^k,
$$

one has

$$
f(n)\;\equiv\;(-1)^n\pmod p.
$$

\subsection{A combinatorial proof via weighted Delannoy paths}

Consider the following \emph{weighted} Delannoy numbers:

$$
f(n)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}\,2^k.
$$

since a path with $k$ diagonals has $\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}$ ways to choose which steps are $D$ and how they intersperse with $E$/$N$.

---

### 1.1.  The involution

We will exhibit a sign-reversing involution on the set of weighted paths, pairing them up so that only "fixed points" survive, and these survivors contribute exactly $(-1)^n$ to the total mod $p$.

1. **Labeling the diagonals.**  Think of each diagonal step $D$ as having two *colors*, say red or blue.  Then each of the $2^k$ weightings of a path with $k$ diagonals can be seen as choosing a coloring of its $k$ diagonals.

2. **Pairing.**  Scan a colored path from start to finish and look for the first time you see either

   * a red diagonal $D_r$, or

   * an $E$ step immediately followed by an $N$ step (an “EN” pattern).

   * **If you see a red diagonal** at position $i$, **change** it to a blue diagonal at the same place.

   * **If you see an “EN” pattern** at positions $(i,i+1)$, replace those two steps by a **blue** diagonal at position $i$ followed by a **red** diagonal at position $i+1$.  (Since blue-then-red still counts as two diagonals, the total number of steps stays $2n$.)

   This operation is clearly involutive: applying it twice returns you to where you started, and it toggles the total number of red diagonals by $\pm1$.

3. **Fixed points.**  A path is *fixed* by this involution precisely if

   * it contains **no** red diagonals, and
   * it never contains an $E$ immediately followed by an $N$.

   The only way to get from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$ with no “EN” patterns is to alternate $N$ and $E$ in the other order---or else use *only* diagonals.  But if you ever use an $E$ or $N$, you eventually force an “EN” somewhere (since you start with, say, $E$ and end up at equal $x$- and $y$-coordinates).  Thus the **only** fixed path is the one consisting of $n$ pure **blue** diagonals in a row.

4. **Weight and congruence.**  That single survivor contributes weight

   $$
     (\text{blue})^n = (+1)^n
     \;=\;(-1)^n
     \pmod p,
   $$

   since blue had been the “$\blank$” choice in our sign-reversing involution.  All other paths cancel in pairs, leaving exactly $(-1)^n$ modulo $p$.

Since the involution paired up everything else,

$$
f(n)\;=\;\sum_{\text{all weighted paths}}1
\;\equiv\;(-1)^n\pmod p.
$$

---

## 2. An algebraic proof via the Frobenius action on the closed form

A classic closed form for the *weighted* Delannoy numbers is obtained by introducing the two roots

$$
\alpha = 1+\sqrt2,\quad \beta=1-\sqrt2
$$

of the quadratic $X^2-2X-1=0$.  One shows by standard generating function or by diagonalization that

$$
f(n)
\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}2^k
\;=\;\frac{\alpha^{2n+1}-\beta^{2n+1}}{\alpha-\beta}
\;=\;\frac{\alpha^{2n+1}-\beta^{2n+1}}{2\sqrt2}.
$$

Because $\alpha\beta=-1$,

$$
\beta^{2n+1}
=\;(-1)^{2n+1}\,\alpha^{-(2n+1)}
=\;-\,\alpha^{-(2n+1)}.
$$

Hence

$$
f(n)
=\frac{\alpha^{2n+1}+\,\alpha^{-(2n+1)}}{2\sqrt2}\;\times\sqrt2
=\frac{\alpha^{2n+1}+\alpha^{-(2n+1)}}2.
$$

---

### 2.1.  Working modulo $p$

We now view everything inside the quadratic extension $\Bbb Z_p[\sqrt2]$.  The key fact is the **Frobenius endomorphism** in characteristic $p$:

$$
\alpha^p \;\equiv\;(1+\sqrt2)^p
\;\equiv\;1^p + (\sqrt2)^p \pmod p
$$

since all the binomial coefficients $\binom p k$ with $0<k<p$ vanish mod $p$.  But $(\sqrt2)^p = (\sqrt2)^{p-1}\sqrt2\equiv\bigl((\sqrt2)^2\bigr)^{\frac{p-1}2}\sqrt2 = 2^{\frac{p-1}2}\sqrt2$.  By Euler's criterion $2^{\frac{p-1}2}\equiv (\tfrac2p)=(-1)^n$, so

$$
(\sqrt2)^p \;\equiv\;(-1)^n\,\sqrt2,
$$

and therefore

$$
\alpha^p \;\equiv\;1 + (-1)^n\sqrt2.
$$

One checks directly that

$$
1+(-1)^n\sqrt2 \;=\;\beta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(-1)^n=-1
\quad\text{or}\quad
(+1)\;=\;+1,
$$

so in fact $\alpha^p\equiv\beta$ and symmetrically $\beta^p\equiv\alpha$.

---

### 2.2.  Completing the argument

Since $p^2=8n+1$, we have

$$
\alpha^{p^2}
\;=\;\bigl(\alpha^p\bigr)^p
\;\equiv\;\beta^p
\;\equiv\;\alpha
\quad(\bmod p),
$$

so $\alpha^{p^2-1}\equiv1$.  But then

$$
\alpha^{2n+1}
\;=\;\alpha^{\frac{p^2-1}4+1}
\;=\;\alpha^{\frac{p^2-1}2}\,\alpha^{-\frac{p^2-1}4+1}
\;\equiv\;(-1)^n\,\alpha^{-\frac{p^2-1}4+1}
$$

and a symmetric relation for $\alpha^{-(2n+1)}$.  Putting these into
$\displaystyle f(n)=\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha^{2n+1}+\alpha^{-(2n+1)}\bigr)$
one finds all dependence on $\alpha^{\pm1}$ cancels, leaving exactly $\tfrac12\bigl((-1)^n+(-1)^n\bigr)=(-1)^n$ mod $p$.

---

## 3.  Conclusion

* The **involution** on colored lattice paths shows combinatorially that *all* contributions cancel in pairs except a single survivor of sign $(-1)^n$.
* The **Frobenius** calculation in $\Bbb Z_p[\sqrt2]$ shows algebraically that the closed-form expression for $f(n)$ collapses to $(-1)^n$ in characteristic $p$.

Either proof gives a deep conceptual reason why

$$
\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+k}{k}\,2^k
\;\equiv\;(-1)^n
\;=\;\bigl(\tfrac2p\bigr)
\;\pmod p.
$$

On elliptic divisibility sequences.

Yes, and the analogy is *surprisingly tight*, once you pick the right “replacement” for
[
u_{n+1}-\alpha u_n=\beta^n,\qquad u_{n+1}-\beta u_n=\alpha^n.
]

For elliptic divisibility sequences (EDS) in the sense of **Morgan Ward**, the role of those two linear identities is played by:

1. a **uniformization / “Binet-type” formula** using the Weierstrass (\sigma)-function, and
2. a single **addition identity** for (\sigma) (equivalently, the “elliptic net” relation), whose specializations *are* Ward's EDS recurrences.

Once you have those, you can derive the Ward recursion, and from that the divisibility/strong divisibility behavior, in much the same spirit as you did for Lucas.

---

## 1) The “Binet law” for EDS: (\sigma(nz)/\sigma(z)^{n^2})

Over (\mathbb C), an elliptic curve (E) has a complex uniformization (\mathbb C/\Lambda \cong E(\mathbb C)). If (P\in E) corresponds to (z\in\mathbb C/\Lambda), then the *normalized division polynomial* (Ward's (F_n), essentially) satisfies
[
F_n(P)=\frac{\sigma(nz;\Lambda)}{\sigma(z;\Lambda)^{n^2}}.
]
Silverman states this explicitly as the bridge from classical elliptic functions to Ward's division polynomials/EDS. 

This is the elliptic-curve analogue of “(\alpha^n)” living in a 1-dimensional algebraic group:

* Lucas: (\alpha^n) is literally an exponential in a quadratic torus.
* Elliptic: (n z) is an exponential in the *elliptic* group, and (\sigma) is the classical gadget that turns the group law into identities among functions.

---

## 2) The “one identity to rule them all”: the elliptic net relation

For Lucas, *one* multiplicativity law (\alpha^{m+n}=\alpha^m\alpha^n) plus the linear extraction (\alpha^n=u_{n+1}-\beta u_n) generates your whole machine.

For EDS, the analogous “one identity” is the **elliptic net (Riemann/Fay-type) relation**. Stange lists it in a clean universal form:
[
\begin{aligned}
& W(p+q+s)W(p-q)W(r+s)W(r)\
&\quad + W(q+r+s)W(q-r)W(p+s)W(p)\
&\quad + W(r+p+s)W(r-p)W(q+s)W(q)=0,
\end{aligned}
]
valid for elliptic nets/EDS. 

A key point: this identity is *exactly* what you get by feeding the (\sigma)-function's addition formulas into the definition (W(n)=\sigma(nz)/\sigma(z)^{n^2}). Conceptually, it is the genus-1 replacement for “exponential addition”.

---

## 3) Ward's EDS recursion as a specialization

Specialize the net relation appropriately and you obtain **Ward's recurrence** (Stange even flags it as “Ward's elliptic divisibility sequences recurrence relation”):
[
W(n+m)W(n-m)W(1)^2
==================

## W(n+1)W(n-1)W(m)^2

W(m+1)W(m-1)W(n)^2.
]


With the usual normalization (W(0)=0, W(1)=1), this becomes the familiar bilinear “addition law” for the sequence.

And, just as with Lucas, you immediately get a small-step recurrence by choosing (m=2):
[
W(n+2)W(n-2)=W(2)^2,W(n+1)W(n-1)-W(3),W(n)^2,
]
again exactly as in the standard EDS literature. 

So: **yes**, there is a fundamental addition law, and it's even more universal than the Lucas addition formula (it's a genuine 4-term bilinear identity rather than a 2-term linear one).

---

## 4) A “Cassini-like” adjacent-term identity (via division polynomials)

For Lucas, Cassini is the invariant determinant/norm that measures how consecutive terms fail to be “rank-1 dependent.”

On an elliptic curve, the *adjacent division polynomials* control the coordinates of (nP). Concretely, with division polynomials (\psi_n),
[
x(nP)=x(P)-\frac{\psi_{n-1}(P)\psi_{n+1}(P)}{\psi_n(P)^2}.
]
([Wikipedia][1])

That formula is very much the elliptic analogue of a Cassini-type statement: it says the ratio (\psi_{n-1}\psi_{n+1}/\psi_n^2) is not “random”, but *is exactly* the correction term needed to jump from (x(P)) to (x(nP)). In practice, it's one of the workhorses behind valuation/gcd arguments for EDS.

---

## 5) Strong divisibility: where it comes from in the elliptic world

There are (at least) two parallel “fundamental sources” of strong divisibility here:

* **Recurrence-definition EDS**: Silverman's notes explicitly record that an EDS given by Ward's recursion is not just a divisibility sequence but a **strong** divisibility sequence:
  [
  W_{\gcd(m,n)}=\gcd(W_m,W_n).
  ]


* **Denominator-definition EDS**: if you write
  [
  x(nP)=\frac{A_n}{D_n^2}\quad(\gcd(A_n,D_n)=1),
  ]
  then the denominator sequence ((D_n)) is itself a **strong divisibility sequence** (and after choosing signs appropriately, it satisfies Ward's EDS recursion). 

Philosophically: in Lucas-land, strong divisibility is “because we're in a 1-dimensional algebraic group with a norm.” In elliptic-land, it's “because we're in a 1-dimensional *abelian variety* with a formal group and well-behaved local valuations of division polynomials / denominators.”
